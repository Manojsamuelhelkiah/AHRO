# Talent management

Understand the benefits and key features of talent management

Factsheet26 Nov, 2025

All locations


Talent management


Talent management refers to the systematic attraction, identification, development, engagement, retention, and deployment of individuals who are valuable to an organisation, because of their potential or the role they have.

To be effective, talent management processes must align with strategic business objectives. Effective, strategic management can deliver several benefits to organisations.  It can help to create a high-performing workplace whilst also increasing productivity, promoting diversity, and providing growth and development opportunities. Effective talent management can also support a strong employer brand.

For these reasons, talent management is a key priority for people professionals. The [CIPD Profession Map](https://www.cipd.org/en/the-people-profession/the-profession-map/) identifies it as a specialist knowledge area and sets out the standards to follow.

This factsheet explores talent management and its benefits.  More information on developing a talent management approach for your organisation is available in our [guide for people professionals](https://www.cipd.org/en/knowledge/guides/talent-management/).

What is talent management?

There are many definitions of the term ‘talent’. We have developed a research-based definition for both ‘talent’ and ‘talent management’:

- Talent refers to individuals who can make a significant difference to organisational performance, either through their immediate contribution or by reaching their potential.
- Talent management is the systematic attraction, identification, development, engagement, retention, and deployment of those individuals who are valuable to an organisation, because of their potential or critical role.

Organisations have different perspectives on their practical approach to talent management. Some focus on high-performing or high-potential individuals.  Others adopt a broader, inclusive approach covering all employees. This definition assumes that everyone has skills and strengths that can be developed and can benefit the organisation. Some take an approach that blends both perspectives: developing all employees, while prioritising a core group.

These definitions highlight that attracting talent alone is insufficient. Effective talent management requires ongoing development and retention, as part of a planned strategy.

The CIPD and Omni RMS 2024 [_Resourcing and talent pl_ **_anning report_**](https://www.cipd.org/en/knowledge/reports/resourcing-surveys/) recommends:

- Taking a strategic approach to workforce planning.
- Removing barriers to work through inclusive recruitment.
- Creating in-house talent development programmes to upskill employees in priority areas.

Benefits of talent management

A strategic approach to managing talent will help organisations prepare for change. As [automation and AI](https://www.cipd.org/en/knowledge/factsheets/ai-technology/) reshape roles, upskilling and re-skilling will become essential.

A coherent talent management strategy directs investment in people and supports broader organisational objectives, including:

- creating meaningful work and growth opportunities
- building a high-performance culture
- promoting [inclusion and diversity](https://www.cipd.org/en/knowledge/factsheets/diversity-factsheet/)
- encouraging continuous learning
- strengthening the ‘employee value proposition’
- using [people analytics](https://www.cipd.org/en/knowledge/factsheets/analytics-factsheet/) for better decision making
- enhancing productivity.

Characteristics of a talent management strategy

An effective talent management strategy must align with organisational strategy if it is to deliver meaningful results.

[Workforce planning](https://www.cipd.org/en/knowledge/factsheets/workforce-planning-factsheet/) is the process of analysing the current workforce and future trends, determining future workforce needs and identifying the gap between the present and the future. Organisations can then create a workforce plan which will help them to prioritise talent management activities, such as:

- recruitment (talent acquisition)
- building talent 'pools'
- succession planning
- life-long learning
- leadership and career development
- performance management
- employee engagement
- employee retention.

CIPD members can find out more in our [strategic workforce planning](https://www.cipd.org/en/knowledge/guides/strategic-workforce-planning/) guide.

**Involving the right people**

There are a range of people involved in talent management, including managers, leaders, HR and those who are participating in talent development programmes. It’s important to include the right stakeholders in deploying a talent management strategy. Senior leaders and managers need to be actively involved in the whole process and make it a top priority.

**Participants**

Managers

Visible senior-level support for talent management is a must. [Line managers](https://www.cipd.org/en/knowledge/factsheets/line-managers-factsheet/) are responsible for employees’ performance and for identifying and developing talent in their own areas. They should also be encouraged to see talent as organisation-wide rather than a local resource.

People teams

People professionals must guide and support the development of talent management approaches. Bringing their expertise, they can play a critical role in facilitating talent pools and programmes and in maintaining their momentum.

Focusing on the talent management loop

The ability to determine and select the most effective methods by which to identify, develop and retain talent is important. The talent management loop identifies six main areas of talent management: attraction, identification, development, engagement, retention and deployment. This can be a useful framework for setting out a talent management strategy.

#### Attracting talent

An attractive [employer brand](https://www.cipd.org/en/knowledge/factsheets/recruitment-brand-factsheet/) and employee value proposition are important in attracting and recruiting new talent. Potential new recruits may be influenced by the public perception of an organisation, as well as its stated values.

#### Identifying talent

This often refers to processes for identifying existing talent within the organisation. A robust [performance management](https://www.cipd.org/en/knowledge/factsheets/performance-factsheet/) process is a way to identify talent early.

[Succession planning](https://www.cipd.org/en/knowledge/factsheets/succession-planning-factsheet/) helps organisations identify and prepare high potential employees for business critical roles or future roles.

Succession planning can include the development of internal ‘talent pools’ of individuals who could step into business-critical or new roles when they arise.

#### Developing talent

Talent can be developed through appropriate learning and development (L&D) initiatives. Typical talent development activities includes coaching, mentoring, formal training, secondments and networking. Some organisations will run talent management programmes that provide exposure to real business issues and engagement with senior leaders. Talent development should align with learning and development initiatives. Find out more on [learning and development strategies](https://www.cipd.org/en/knowledge/factsheets/strategy-development-factsheet/) that align with business objectives.

#### Engaging talent

Employees who have [good quality jobs](https://www.cipd.org/en/knowledge/reports/goodwork/), autonomy, clarity of expectations, and are well-managed, are usually happier, healthier and more fulfilled. They are also more likely to perform well. This mutual gains view of motivation and people management lies at the heart of [employee engagement](https://www.cipd.org/en/knowledge/factsheets/engagement-factsheet/).

#### Retaining talent

Investing in development and engagement activities can help to reduce [employee turnover](https://www.cipd.org/en/knowledge/factsheets/turnover-retention-factsheet/) and enhance retention. Reward (including pay and benefits) and recognition can also aid talent retention. Approaches to reward should offer a mix of rewards. It might include incentives and acknowledgement of an individual’s value to the organisation. More information is available in our [factsheet](https://www.cipd.org/en/knowledge/factsheets/reward-factsheet/) on Reward.

#### Deploying talent

Deployment is using the workforce in the most effective and efficient way. Through workforce planning, organisations can identify skills gaps in their workforce, which can then inform learning and development plans. Talent can be deployed through job rotations, skill enhancement opportunities, formal learning, project work and secondments.

Tracking and evaluating talent management

The outcomes of talent management activities should always be reviewed to analyse return on investment. More information on this can be found in our [talent management guide for people professionals](https://www.cipd.org/en/knowledge/guides/talent-management/).

There is also more information in our factsheets on [people analytics](https://www.cipd.org/en/knowledge/factsheets/analytics-factsheet/) and [workforce reporting](https://www.cipd.org/en/knowledge/factsheets/workforce-reporting-factsheet/). Our [Valuing your talent](https://www.cipd.org/en/views-and-insights/thought-leadership/insight/valing-talent/) research programme provides a framework and further resources. These include a diagnostic tool to assess how organisations measure human capital.

Ultimately, organisational success is the one of the strongest indicators of effective talent management.
